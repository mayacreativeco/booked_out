How to raise your rates without losing clients.

The signals that tell you it's time to raise — and the exact script to do it without burning the relationship.

// signals_it_is_time

→
70%+ of your pitches convert. High conversion means you're underpriced. The market is telling you your floor is too low.
→
You're booked 4+ weeks in advance. If your schedule is full, demand exceeds supply. That's called pricing power — use it.
→
You have real results to point to. ROAS numbers, view counts, conversion data — any of this justifies a rate increase anchored in value, not just time.
→
6+ months have passed since your last raise. Inflation, demand, and your own skill level all go up over time. Your rate should too.

// the_raise_script · for_existing_clients

Hi [Name],

I wanted to give you a heads-up that my rates are going up on [DATE] — I'm raising my standard package to $[NEW RATE].

Because of how well our projects have gone, I want to offer you a transition window: any project booked before [DATE] will be at the current rate of $[CURRENT RATE].

Do you have anything you'd want to lock in?

— [Your name]

// give 30 days notice. offer a transition window. the urgency closes the deal before the rate changes — you get revenue and they get value.

// how_much_to_raise

first_raise

Raise 20–25%. Large enough to matter, small enough to keep most clients. Re-run the Rate Calculator under "established" to see your new range.

ongoing_raises

15–20% every 6–9 months as long as you're consistently booking. Smaller, more frequent raises are easier to absorb than large jumps.

premium_raise

When you have documented results (ROAS, conversion data), raise 40–50% and anchor the increase to the value you've proven — not the time you've put in.

// tool_02 · retainer_roadmap

How to convert a brand into a retainer.

Project work is unpredictable income. Retainers are predictable. Here's how to move a brand from "one-off deal" to "ongoing partner."

01
Deliver beyond scope on the first project.

Overdeliver once, on purpose. An extra cut they didn't ask for. A concept they can use but didn't commission. This isn't free work — it's a calculated investment in relationship equity that opens the retainer conversation.

02
Pitch the retainer at delivery, not before.

When you deliver final cuts, include: "I'd love to build on this for Q[X]. I'm putting together a monthly package for [2-3 brands I trust] — want me to send a brief on what that would look like for [brand]?" Timing matters. Pitch when they're happiest with you.

03
Structure the retainer as "monthly creative partner."

Not "monthly content package." The language shift positions you as a strategic asset, not a vendor. A simple retainer structure:

→ 4 × 30-sec videos / month
→ 2 unique hook directions each
→ 30 days paid social usage per batch
→ Priority turnaround (7 days vs. standard 14)
→ Monthly strategy call (30 min)
→ $[RATE × 3.2 / month] · 3-month commitment
04
Require a 3-month minimum. Never month-to-month.

Month-to-month retainers get cancelled the moment something tightens at the brand. A 3-month commitment gives you predictability and gives them commitment to actually use what they're paying for. It also gives you enough runway to produce work that actually shows results.

// tool_03 · brand_psychology

How brands actually make buying decisions.

Understanding what's happening on the brand side explains why certain pitches land and certain deals stall. Use this to read situations faster.

insight_01
The person you're pitching isn't spending their own money.

They're spending a budget, and they're managing risk. Your job isn't to make the spend feel exciting — it's to make the spend feel safe. "Here's what I've done before, here's what I'll do for you, here's exactly what's included" eliminates risk. Risk elimination closes deals.

insight_02
Brands have a social proof problem at every stage.

They need to defend the spend upward. When you give them a results number, a recognizable brand reference, or a clear deliverable, you're giving them ammunition to take to their boss. Pitches that include "my work for [comparable brand] drove X" are easy to approve. Abstract pitches are not.

insight_03
Timing is a bigger factor than quality.

Most "no"s aren't "no, never" — they're "no, not right now." A brand that ignored you in January might close in March because they just kicked off a new quarter, launched a new product, or hired a new marketing manager. Follow-up cadence matters more than pitch perfection.

insight_04
Brands don't fully understand UGC pricing — yet.

Most brands have no benchmark for what UGC should cost. If you anchor high with a clear justification (usage rights, turnaround, deliverable count), they'll accept that number because they have nothing to compare it to. The first number you say sets the frame. Say it with confidence.

insight_05
The relationship matters more than the deliverable.

The brand contacts who keep coming back are the ones who feel like working with you was easy — not just that the content was good. Be responsive, be clear, be professional. "Easy to work with" is a competitive advantage at every rate level.

insight_06
Most brands are running too many tests at once.

Their attention is split. Your proposal should be the easiest decision in their inbox that day. Short scope. One deliverable structure. One rate. One next step. Brands close proposals that require one reply, not five.

// tool_04 · operator_faq

Operator FAQ.

The weird questions I get asked most — the ones that don't fit anywhere else but matter when you're running the business at this level.

Should I form an LLC before I start charging? +
Short answer: not immediately. Operate as a sole proprietor under your own name until you're consistently clearing ~$30K/year from UGC work. At that point, an LLC (with an S-corp election in the US) starts saving you meaningful tax dollars. Before that, the paperwork and state fees aren't worth it. Disclaimer: I'm not an accountant — this is just what I did. Talk to one before you file anything.
Do I need a contract for every deal? +
Yes. Every single one. Even the $500 ones. The proposal template in Get Consistent can double as a lightweight SOW when signed. Use HelloSign or PandaDoc to make it one-click on their side.
How do I handle taxes as a creator? +
Set aside 25-30% of every deposit into a separate high-yield savings account labeled "taxes" and don't touch it. File quarterly estimated taxes if you're in the US and earning more than $1K/quarter from UGC. Hire an accountant who has at least 3 other creator clients — it's worth the $400-800/year.
What if a brand wants me to use their product in a way I don't believe in? +
Walk. Every single time. The content won't perform (because your delivery will be off), the brand will blame you, and your audience will sniff it out. Your portfolio is worth more than any one deal. The recovery scripts include a polite exit note you can send.
How do I handle a brand that keeps scope-creeping after signing? +
Use the scope-creep script in the Negotiation Playbook (scenario 03). Frame every new request as an add-on or a swap — never as "free extra work". One warning then the add-on invoice goes out regardless. Brands respect clear boundaries.
When should I raise my rates? +
When more than 70% of your pitches are converting, it's time to raise. High conversion = you're under-priced. Raise the floor by 20-25% and watch what happens. You'll lose the cheapest deals and keep the ones that actually move the business. Re-run the rate calculator under "established" to see your new numbers.
How do I know if I'm ready to scale? +
You're ready to scale when you're consistently booked (not just sometimes), you have a real pipeline (not just one deal at a time), and you're turning down work because you're at capacity — not because you're scared. If any of those three are missing, go back to Get Consistent and run the CEO dashboard for 60 more days before making structural changes.
